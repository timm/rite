# ste

## Short form

- Two parts: writing rules + a controlled dictionary
  (about 900 approved words, one meaning each, one part
  of speech each; American spelling; when synonyms
  exist, STE keeps one: "start", not "begin, commence,
  initiate, originate").
- Hard numbers: procedural sentences <= 20 words;
  descriptive sentences and notes <= 25 words;
  paragraphs <= 6 sentences, one topic each, topic
  sentence first; multi-word noun clusters <= 3 words.
- Verbs: only infinitive, imperative, simple present,
  simple past, simple future, and past participle as
  adjective. No perfect tenses, no progressives, no
  "-ing" verb forms (an "-ing" word is legal only inside
  a technical noun). Active voice; passive only in
  description when the agent is unknown.
- Procedures: imperative commands, one instruction per
  sentence (two only if simultaneous); condition first,
  then a comma, then the command.
- Safety: WARNING (injury) vs CAUTION (damage); start
  with command or condition; then state the risk.
- Keep articles ("Turn THE shaft assembly"); use
  connecting words (and, but, then, thus, as a result)
  to link sentences; reuse key words verbatim, never
  elegant variation.

## The rules (Part 1, Issue 9), by section

Sections 1-7 below are the specification's own summary
boxes, near verbatim.

### 1 Words
1.1 Use words that are approved in the dictionary,
    technical nouns, or technical verbs.
1.2 Use approved words only as the specified part of
    speech.
1.3 Use approved words only with their approved
    meanings.
1.4 Use only the approved forms of verbs and
    adjectives.
1.5 You can use words that fit a technical noun
    category.
1.6 Use an unapproved word only when it is (part of) a
    technical noun.
1.7 Do not use technical nouns as verbs.
1.8 Use technical nouns approved in your company,
    industry, or subject field.
1.9 When you select a technical noun, use one that is
    short and easy to understand.
1.10 No regional, slang, or jargon words as technical
    nouns.
1.11 Do not use different technical nouns for the same
    item.
1.12 You can use verbs that fit a technical verb
    category.
1.13 Do not use technical verbs as nouns.
1.14 American English spelling.

### 2 Multi-word nouns
2.1 No noun clusters of more than three words.
    Break long clusters with prepositions: "calibration
    of the resistance of the runway light connection".
2.2 A technical noun longer than three words: write it
    in full at first use, then give a shorter form, or
    hyphenate words that act as one unit.

### 3 Verbs
3.1 Use only the verb forms given in the dictionary.
3.2 Only: infinitive, imperative, simple present,
    simple past, simple future, past participle as
    adjective.
3.3 Past participle only as an adjective (before a
    noun, or after be/become/stay).
3.4 No auxiliary verbs making complex constructions
    (no "has adjusted", "is to be installed", "must be
    adjusted" -> "adjust").
3.5 "-ing" form only inside a technical noun.
3.6 Active voice. (Descriptive text: passive only when
    the agent is unknown.)
3.7 Describe an action with an approved verb, not a
    noun ("do a test" or "test", never nominalize the
    action away).

### 4 Sentences
4.1 One topic per sentence.
4.2 Do not omit words to shorten sentences (no
    telegraphic style).
4.3 Use vertical lists for complex text; items must
    connect grammatically to the lead-in.
4.4 Use connecting words and phrases (and, but, then,
    thus, as a result, this/these) to link sentences
    with related topics.
4.5 When applicable, use an article (the, a, an) or a
    demonstrative adjective (this, these) before a
    noun. Exception: no article before general
    statements, abstract concepts, or nouns followed by
    an alphanumeric identifier.

### 5 Procedural writing
5.1 Sentences <= 20 words (notes are exempt: 25).
5.2 One instruction per sentence, unless the actions
    are simultaneous.
5.3 Imperative (command) form. "Must" only for safety
    or an important condition.
5.4 A condition the reader must know comes first, then
    a comma, then the command.
5.5 Notes give information, never instructions.

### 6 Descriptive writing
6.1 Give information gradually.
6.2 Use key words and key phrases for logical
    structure; never vary them.
6.3 Sentences <= 25 words.
6.4 Use paragraphs to group related information; topic
    sentence first.
6.5 One topic per paragraph.
6.6 No paragraph over six sentences.

### 7 Safety instructions
7.1 Name the risk level: warning = injury or death;
    caution = damage to objects. Both risks -> warning.
7.2 Start with a clear command or condition.
7.3 Then give an explanation that shows the risk or
    possible result.

### 8 Punctuation and word count (summarized, not
verbatim)
Semicolons rare; dashes and parentheses have narrow
uses; hyphenated words count as one word; numbers plus
units count per the spec; headings, titles, and
acronyms have their own rules (8.6); acronyms are
defined at first use.

### 9 Writing practices and general recommendations
(summarized, not verbatim)
When a word replacement fails, use a different sentence
construction, not a synonym hunt (9.1). Check approved
meanings (9.2). Keep one consistent style (9.4).
GR-1 "that": keep it to introduce clauses. GR-2 careful
with "with". GR-3 pronouns sparingly. GR-4 "this" +
noun. GR-5 beware false friends. GR-6 no Latin
abbreviations (e.g., i.e., etc.). GR-7 inclusive
language. GR-8 avoid the possessive apostrophe.

## Applying STE to research prose

- Rewrite, do not decorate: split every sentence over
  the limit at its clause joints; give each half its
  own verb.
- Kill nominalizations first ("perform an evaluation
  of" -> "evaluate"); they cause most limit violations.
- Quoted text and proper nouns are exempt; citations
  and math are invisible to the word count.
- STE conflicts with some house styles (STE welcomes
  sentence-initial "But"/"And" as connecting words;
  some registers ban them). State which contract wins
  in the document that uses it.
- Dictionary discipline without the dictionary: prefer
  the shortest common word; use each chosen word for
  exactly one meaning across the text; never use two
  words for one thing.
